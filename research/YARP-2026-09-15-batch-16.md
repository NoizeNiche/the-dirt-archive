# YARP 2026-09-15 Batch 16

## Scope

This batch advances the catalog-completion phase for Bell Electrolabs, Gretsch, Moreschi, Electronic Sounds, fOXX, and related historical European/American lines. Primary period advertisements are preferred where recovered; specialist sources are used to identify product relationships and unresolved factory questions.

## 1. Bell Electrolabs: catalog structure now materially stronger

A 1978 International Musician advertisement confirms the Bell Modular Effects System as an active commercial product line and explicitly names Phaser, Fuzz, Sustain, Vibrato, Flanger and ADT modules. The advertisement identifies Bell Electrolabs at Reeds Farm, Stokenchurch, Bucks.

Black Guitars' historical work adds a broader model chronology: models introduced by 1977 included Mother, Sustain, Fuzz, Flanger, Vibrato, Envelope Follower, Multi-Phaser, Phaser, Voice Box and Voice Bag; later models included Analogue Echo and Programmable Filter. The same source documents changes in Bell artwork and manufacturing materials through the late 1970s and early 1980s.

For the central dirt catalog, Fuzz and Sustain are the primary records. Mother is retained as a system/power boundary record rather than dirt. The remaining Bell modules should not inflate the dirt count.

Sources:
- https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/International-Musician/70s/International-Musician-1978-08.pdf
- https://blackguitars.com/bell-electrolabs.html

## 2. Gretsch effects range: separate the marketed product family

New Cut Studios documents the early-1970s Gretsch range as including Expandafuzz, Controfuzz, Tremofect, Freq Faze, and Tone Divider, with the Expandafuzz incorporating the Controfuzz fuzz circuit plus additional tone/effect sections. The same source connects the Gretsch Playboy Series to JEN manufacture for several products, including Harmon Booster and Jumbo Fuzz, but keeps the Expandafuzz-era American units distinct.

Tone Machines Blog independently dates the Expandafuzz to 1973 and emphasizes the uncertainty around its factory, including unproven Maestro/JEN possibilities. The archive therefore uses the following relationship rule: JEN is a documented OEM for specific Gretsch Playboy products, but this does not transfer automatically to Expandafuzz or Controfuzz.

Sources:
- https://www.newcutstudios.co.uk/kicking-pedals-and-casting-pearls-before-sine-waves
- https://www.tonemachinesblog.com/2012/06/gretsch-expandafuzz-1973.html

## 3. Moreschi catalog expansion

Moreschi now has at least two separate dirt-related product nodes: Wah-Fuzz and Octave Fuzz. Effects Database identifies the Octave Fuzz as a rare Italian-made 1970s model associated with Gary Hurst and Cav. Giovanni Moreschi in Castelfidardo. Amplified Parts independently connects Moreschi's Wah-Fuzz to Gary Hurst's Electronic Sounds UFO Wah Fuzz Swell family.

The archive should retain the designer relationship to Gary Hurst while keeping Moreschi as the marketed/Italian manufacturer entity. Octave Fuzz and Wah-Fuzz are distinct models and should not be collapsed into a single generic Moreschi Hurst pedal.

Sources:
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/moreschi/octavefuzz
- https://www.amplifiedparts.com/tech-articles/moreschi-wah-fuzz-teardown

## 4. Electronic Sounds / Gary Hurst Italian corridor

Effects Database identifies the Electronic Sounds UFO Octa-Fuzz as a Gary Hurst-designed early-1970s product and links it to the CBS-Arbiter Doubler and Moreschi Octave Fuzz. This establishes an important product-family relationship for the archive without requiring the archive to publish circuit-level material.

Electronic Sounds should be treated as a distinct marketed/product lineage around Hurst's later designs. The Italian manufacturing question should remain model-specific until period company records are found. The archive should not simply convert the Electronic Sounds name into a universal Moreschi or other factory attribution.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/electronicsounds/garyhurst/ufo/octafuzz

## 5. fOXX / Footsystem / Turtle family clarification

Tonehome identifies Ridinger Associates Inc. in Chatsworth, California as maker of the fOXX Clean Machine and dates a surviving example to 1973. Effects Database records Footsystem FS300 as a rebadged Clean Machine and the broader family includes Turtle Clean Machine.

The archive now treats these as one manufacturer/product-family cluster with distinct retail identities. This is an example where OEM/rebadge consolidation is supported strongly enough to reduce duplicate builder inflation, while preserving separate searchable names.

Sources:
- https://www.tonehome.de/foxx/clean-machine/
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/footsystem/fs300

## 6. Blackfield catalog caution

Tonehome's Blackfield page adds a second, non-Schaller product family to the Blackfield entity through the Flying Sound Fuzz/Wah/Phaser, dated approximately 1971. Effects Database separately identifies a Blackfield Fuzz as an OEM version of the Schaller Fuzz.

This combination means Blackfield should not be represented solely as a Schaller rebrand. The Blackfield Fuzz and Flying Sound remain separate product families with different evidence relationships.

Sources:
- https://www.tonehome.de/blackfield/
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/blackfield/fuzz

## Evidence rules reinforced

- A documented OEM relationship for one Gretsch product family does not automatically cover every Gretsch effects model.
- Gary Hurst designer lineage is separate from physical manufacturing attribution.
- A rebadge cluster can be consolidated only when model-level evidence supports it, preserving the retail identities as aliases/relationship nodes.
- A single manufacturer can have both OEM-branded products and apparently independent original products; Blackfield is a useful example.
