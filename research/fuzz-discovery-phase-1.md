# The Dirt Archive — Fuzz Discovery Phase 1

## Purpose

This research file is the working census behind the Dirt Archive's fuzz section. It is a discovery and prioritization document, not a claim of literal completeness. The web contains unindexed pages, dead builders, one-off builds, private commissions, OEM relationships, renamed companies and contradictory oral histories. The archive therefore records what can be found and verified, then keeps an explicit research queue for what still needs work.

## Editorial boundary

The archive documents historical identity, production history, external identifiers, provenance, and meaningful technical distinctions that help identify production periods. It does **not** publish gutshot galleries, schematics, PCB layouts, complete bills of materials, or cloning instructions.

A component distinction may be recorded when it is historically meaningful and diagnostically useful, for example a documented transition from germanium to silicon or a builder-confirmed resistor technology change. The archive should not publish a complete component recipe merely because one is available elsewhere.

## Core historical discovery source

Fuzzboxes.org's timeline is especially valuable for early British fuzz history. Its dating methodology explicitly treats dates as estimates based on surviving pedals, part date codes, press advertisements and witness testimony, and notes that estimates may change as new evidence appears. The timeline identifies, among others, Maestro Fuzz-Tone (1962), Gary Hurst Tone Bender MKI and GP Electronics Harmonic Generator (c.1964), Tone Bender MK1.5 and WEM Rush Pep Box (1965), Arbiter Fuzz Face, Carlsbro Fuzz-Tone, John Hornby Skewes Zonk Machine, Baldwin-Burns Buzzaround, Selmer Buzz Tone, Rotosound Fuzz Box and Sola Sound Tone Bender Professional MKII (1966), Vox Professional MKII and Zonk II (1967), Vox MKIII and Jennings Fuzz (1968), WEM Project V, John Hornby Skewes Shatterbox, Park Fuzz Sound and Sola Sound MKIV (1969–1970).

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

## Fuzz Face research notes

Fuzzboxes documents four principal original enclosure generations for Arbiter/Dallas-Arbiter Fuzz Faces and ties those generations to graphic changes, knob styles and transistor families. The third enclosure with the narrow central brow appears around 1968–1969. The fourth enclosure appears around 1969–1970 and remained in use until UK production ended in 1976. Decal graphics are associated with the 1970s; early examples used screen printing.

Fuzzboxes also notes a strong correlation between early 'top hat' knobs and certain germanium examples, while later round 'Orange'-style knobs are associated with 1970s silicon production. Some anomalies exist, so appearance alone should not be treated as an exact year decoder.

Useful archive distinctions:
- Enclosure generation
- Branding: Arbiter / Dallas-Arbiter / CBS-Arbiter / Dallas Music Industries
- Graphic method: silk-screen vs decal
- Knob style
- Footswitch style
- Germanium vs silicon technology
- Production-date evidence from surviving specimens

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/fuzzface
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/arbiter/fuzzface

## Tone Bender / Sola Sound genealogy notes

The Tone Bender should be treated as a **family**, not a single linear product. Fuzzboxes identifies the Sola Sound MKI, MK1.5, Professional MKII, MKIII and MKIV and documents OEM relationships to Vox, Rotosound, Park, Marshall, Rangemaster and John Hornby Skewes.

Key evidence examples:
- Gary Hurst began developing Tone Bender in 1964; MKI appeared by 1965.
- Earliest known MK1.5 examples date to 1965; its exact overlap with late MKI production remains uncertain.
- Professional MKII was announced in late 1966.
- Vox Professional MKII was Sola Sound-built during 1967–1968.
- Vox MKIII appeared in 1968 and continued through the 1970s.
- Park Fuzz Sound appears in 1969 in a Tone Bender MKIII-related form.
- Sola Sound MKIV appears around 1970; MKIII and MKIV enclosures could overlap during the 1970s.
- The MKIV 'Batman' graphics appear around 1971, and the model designation was later removed from the artwork.

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline
- https://fuzzboxes.org/features/tonebenders
- https://fuzzboxes.org/tonebendermkiv
- https://fuzzboxes.org/parkfuzzsound
- https://fuzzboxes.org/marshallsupafuzz
- https://fuzzboxes.org/shatterbox

## Big Muff research notes

Kit Rae's Big Muff History is the principal secondary research source for detailed Big Muff chronology. Rae explicitly states that his version, edition and revision numbers are his identification categories rather than official Electro-Harmonix identifiers.

Major research branches currently recognized by the source include:
- Triangle Big Muff
- Ram's Head Big Muff
- Red/Black Big Muff
- Op-Amp Big Muff
- later U.S. Big Muff generations
- Sovtek / Russian production families
- NYC Classic reissues
- Green Russian reissue
- Ram's Head reissue
- artist and promotional editions
- OEM/rebranded Big Muff products

The historical record also demonstrates that one named generation can contain many circuit variants without those necessarily being separate consumer-facing products. The archive should represent these as internal production distinctions or claims only where they materially help identification.

## Japanese / OEM fuzz discovery

A major future branch is the Japanese OEM ecosystem, where one underlying product can appear under many labels. Effects Database's Teisco Fuzz Machine entry states that the product was distributed under names including Ibanez Standard Fuzz (model 59), Bruno Fuzz Machine, Mica Fuzz, Marlboro Wailer Fuzz, Antoria Fuzz Machine and Aria Diamond Fuzz, while the related Teisco TF-1 Fuzz is documented as a rebranded FY-6 Super Fuzz family product.

This is an important model for our database:

**OEM lineage != retail brand != model family != physical specimen**

Sources:
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/teisco/fuzzmachine
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/teisco/tf1
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/teisco

## Modern boutique discovery targets

Effects Database currently exposes substantial product families for many boutique builders. These are discovery starting points, not yet complete variation research.

### Devi Ever FX
Effects Database currently lists 75 products and notes that Devi Ever founded the business in late 2003 under Effector 13, licensed the original company name and some designs to Ooh La La Manufacturing in 2008, then regained rights after two years. This creates an especially important builder/entity timeline for the archive.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/deviever

### Basic Audio
Effects Database currently lists 27 products and identifies John Lyons as the owner/operator, with his timeline stating he started making pedals in 1998.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/basicaudio

### Monsterpiece Fuzz
Effects Database currently lists 20 products, including MkI, Mk1.5, MkII, MkIII, Classic, Ge/Si families, NPN/PNP families, Jr. versions and Supa Fuzz. This is a good example of a builder where model naming itself encodes meaningful technology or lineage distinctions.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/monsterpiece

### Blackout Effectors
Effects Database currently lists 17 products and records the company's founding by Kyle Tompkins in Vancouver in 2008 followed by a move to Asheville in 2009. Dirt products include Fix'D Fuzz, Fix'D Fuzz Deluxe, FUBÄR, Musket Fuzz, Blunderbuss and multiple Twosome products.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/blackout

### Stomp Under Foot
Effects Database currently lists 30 products, including extensive Big Muff and Tone Bender-related products. The page identifies Matt Pasquerella as the builder and includes a builder timeline beginning with organized pedal production in summer 2006.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/stompunderfoot

### Wren and Cuff
Effects Database currently lists 45 products and includes multiple vintage-family reinterpretations. Builder interviews are also indexed, making it a strong candidate for a complete builder catalog pass.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/wrencuff

### Fuzzrocious
Effects Database currently lists 43 products. Dirt examples include 420 Fuzz, Blast Furnace, Cicada Fuzz, Green Stache, Grey Stache, Grey Stache Plus, Demon King, Feed Me and collaboration products.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/fuzzrocious

### Death By Audio
Effects Database has a substantial catalog and explicitly distinguishes named revisions such as Octave Clang v2 and Waveformer Destroyer Mk2. This makes it a useful target for production/revision research.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/deathbyaudio

### ZVEX
Effects Database currently lists 69 products. Important fuzz research candidates include Fuzz Factory, Fuzz Factory 7, Fat Fuzz Factory, Woolly Mammoth, Fuzz Probe and the Vertical Fuzz Factory. ZVEX's own site and catalog distinguish hand-painted and Vexter production, which is exactly the kind of production distinction this archive should preserve.

Sources:
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/zvex
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/zvex/fuzzprobe
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/zvex/vertical/fuzzfactory
- https://www.zvex.com/

### Catalinbread
Effects Database currently lists 124 products. Fuzz-related examples include Antichthon, Blood Donor, CB Fuzz, Giygas, Giygas 2K, Karma Suture Germanium, Karma Suture Silicon, Katzenkönig, Knight School Fuzz, Manx Loaghtan, Merkin, Ottava Magus, Perseus, and other effects with fuzz/drive overlap.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/catalinbread

## Discovery methodology for the next phases

1. Use broad indexes to discover candidate builders and products.
2. Use builder/brand pages to establish model names.
3. Prefer original builder documents and contemporary material for dates and product claims.
4. Use surviving-specimen research to establish production periods and physical identifiers.
5. Record disputed claims explicitly instead of resolving them by repetition.
6. Only promote a variation to 'Confirmed' after sufficient evidence is found.
7. Keep collector-submitted examples as specimens even when they do not change the public version taxonomy.

## Archive rule emerging from Phase 1

The strongest structure is:

**Builder / designer entity**
→ **family**
→ **retail model**
→ **generation**
→ **production period**
→ **edition**
→ **specimen**
→ **claims + evidence**

This should remain the canonical model for the Dirt Archive.
