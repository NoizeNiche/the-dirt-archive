# The Dirt Archive — Fuzz Census Phase 2: Vintage & OEM Candidates

## Status

Research pass focused on the earliest commercial fuzz era and the British/OEM ecosystem. This file intentionally preserves sparse and disputed records instead of filtering them out.

## Evidence rule

Dates in early fuzz history are frequently estimates. Fuzzboxes explains that its dates combine surviving-pedal part date codes, press advertisements and witness testimony, and are subject to revision as new examples surface. The Dirt Archive should use the same principle: approximate dates are preferable to invented precision.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

## 1962 — Maestro / Gibson

### Maestro Fuzz-Tone FZ-1

The FZ-1 is identified by Fuzzboxes as the first commercial fuzz box, released by Gibson in 1962. It was primarily sold in the U.S. and was imported into the U.K. by Selmer. Effects Database's Maestro index also lists later Maestro FZ-1A, FZ-1B, FZ-1S and FZ-2 records.

Archive work needed:
- Separate Gibson-era product identity from later Maestro brand histories.
- Establish FZ-1 / FZ-1A / FZ-1B distinctions.
- Identify enclosure, control, label and production-period changes.
- Separate original products from modern Maestro Original Collection reissues.

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline
- https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/maestro
- https://brandbook.gibson.com/overview/maestro/

## 1964–1965 — early British fuzz

### Gary Hurst / Sola Sound Tone Bender MKI

Fuzzboxes dates development to 1964 and commercial availability to 1965. Early examples used wooden enclosures; later examples used folded-steel enclosures.

Archive priority: P1

Identification clues to document:
- enclosure material and shape
- branding
- graphics
- control count/layout
- early/late construction
- association with Macari's Musical Exchange

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### G. P. Electronics Harmonic Generator

Fuzzboxes identifies G. P. Electronics of Bovey Tracey, Devon as the designer of an early British fuzz box around 1964. The Baldwin-Burns Buzzaround is described by Fuzzboxes as a copy of this design.

Archive priority: P1 because the surviving product history is sparse but historically important.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Sola Sound Tone Bender MK1.5

Earliest known examples date to 1965. Fuzzboxes notes strong anecdotal evidence for shop availability by early 1966 while leaving open the possibility of overlap with late MKI production.

Archive rule: do not treat “MK1.5” as necessarily a manufacturer-assigned revision number. Record it as an archival/research family name unless primary documentation establishes otherwise.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### WEM Rush Pep Box

An early British fuzz invented by Pepe Rush and later marketed by WEM. Fuzzboxes says WEM was already involved with promotion and distribution by 1965, but the exact origin date of Rush's design is unknown.

Archive priority: P1

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Vox Distortion Booster

Red Vox Distortion Boosters were made in the U.K. and demonstrated at the Russell Hotel trade show in August 1965. This is not automatically a “fuzz” record in every taxonomy, so the archive should classify it based on historical marketing and sound-function evidence rather than force it into the fuzz bucket.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

## 1966 — the first explosion of British fuzz production

### Dallas-Arbiter Fuzz Face

First demonstrated at a trade fair in August 1966. Fuzzboxes describes the Fuzz Face as derived from the Tone Bender MK1.5 and notes U.K. production through 1976.

Key identification work:
- first/second/third/fourth enclosure generations
- Arbiter versus Dallas-Arbiter versus CBS/Arbiter versus Dallas Music Industries branding
- screen-printed versus decal graphics
- knob families
- footswitch families
- germanium versus silicon periods
- 1966–1976 production chronology

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/fuzzface

### Carlsbro Fuzz-Tone

Carlsbro of Nottingham advertised the Fuzz-Tone in May 1966. Fuzzboxes identifies it as a short-lived three-transistor pedal. The Carlsbro page also preserves company-history sources and owner communications used in the site's research.

Archive priority: P1

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline
- https://fuzzboxes.org/carlsbrofuzztone

### John Hornby Skewes Zonk Machine

The Zonk Machine was first mentioned in the press in early 1966 and is described as a copy of the Tone Bender MKI. It remained in production at least until 1968.

Archive work:
- enclosure/graphics generations
- relationship to Sola Sound/Wilsic Sound
- distinguish Zonk Machine from Zonk II

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Baldwin-Burns Buzzaround

Appeared on a Burns price list dated April 1966 and was made in reasonable quantities through at least 1969. Fuzzboxes describes it as a copy of the G. P. Harmonic Generator.

Archive priority: P1

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Selmer Buzz Tone

Selmer imported Maestro products into Britain and developed its own Buzz Tone by August 1966, based on the American design.

Archive work:
- distinguish imported Maestro history from Selmer-designed product
- establish production window and external variants

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Rotosound Fuzz Box

Rotosound sold fuzz boxes from 1966 until approximately the early 1970s. Fuzzboxes states that Sola Sound built/supplied the products and that several versions of the Tone Bender were used under Rotosound branding.

Archive priority: P1 because this is an archetypal OEM/brand relationship.

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline
- https://fuzzboxes.org/rotosoundfuzzbox

### Sola Sound Tone Bender Professional MKII

Successor to the MK1.5, announced in the press in late 1966. Fuzzboxes describes it as a modified version of Sola Sound's earlier two-transistor fuzz box.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Rangemaster Fuzzbug

A short-lived fuzz product supplied by Sola Sound to J. E. Dallas & Sons under the Rangemaster name.

Archive priority: P1 due to its OEM significance.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Marshall SupaFuzz

First appeared in 1966. Early versions were supplied by Sola Sound; Marshall took over production in 1968. Fuzzboxes distinguishes the Sola Sound and Marshall production eras and documents later enclosure changes.

Useful archival distinctions:
- Sola Sound / early angular enclosure
- Marshall cast enclosure from 1968
- screen-printed versus embossed lettering
- early versus later knob styles
- 1970s production confirmation by potentiometer dates

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/marshallsupafuzz
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Sperrin Fuzz Box

Developed by David Sperrin in Southport and advertised repeatedly in 1966. Fuzzboxes states that details are especially scarce because no surviving examples are known to have been discovered.

Archive status: Preserve as a sparse historical record, not as a normal fully documented product.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Kelly Amplification Fuzz Box

Advertised in May 1966 but almost entirely undocumented due to the absence of known surviving examples.

Archive status: Preserve as an evidence-limited record.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

## 1967

### Vox Tone Bender Professional MKII

Sola Sound-built during 1967–1968. Fuzzboxes says it used the same three-transistor Tone Bender MKII circuit Sola Sound was supplying to other brands.

Archive relationship:
**Retail brand:** Vox
**Builder/OEM:** Sola Sound
**Family:** Tone Bender Professional MKII

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### John Hornby Skewes Zonk II

Available by 1967. Initially housed in cast aluminium enclosures and supplied by Sola Sound; by 1969 production was taken over by Wilsic Sound.

This is an excellent specimen for the archive's builder/brand/production relationship model.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

## 1968

### Vox Tone Bender MKIII

Successor to the Professional MKII, released in 1968 and manufactured by Sola Sound through the 1970s.

Research should distinguish:
- two-knob examples
- germanium three-control forms
- other documented production forms
- later silicon variants
- Italian Vox/Tone Bender products that share branding but not U.K. manufacture

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline
- https://fuzzboxes.org/features/tonebenders

### Jennings Fuzz

Tom Jennings formed Jennings Electronic Developments/Industries after leaving JMI; together with Dick Denney, the company offered distinctive fuzz boxes.

Archive priority: P2 until product-level primary documentation is expanded.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

## 1969–1970

### WEM Project V

First appeared in product catalogues in 1969 and is described by Fuzzboxes as a relatively sophisticated early fuzz box.

Archive priority: P1/P2

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### John Hornby Skewes Shatterbox

A multi-effect fuzz box combining the Zonk II and Treble Booster. Fuzzboxes reports a first public unveiling at the Frankfurt Musikmesse in spring 1969 and says the earliest surviving examples use stripboard, while early-1970s production moved to PCB construction with slightly revised graphics.

Important archival distinction:
- stripboard era
- PCB era
- graphics change
- international distribution evidence

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/shatterbox

### B&M Exel Shatterbox

Barnes & Mullins marketed a Shatterbox in its Exel range; Fuzzboxes places production around 1969 through the early 1970s.

Archive priority: P2, pending product-level research.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Park Fuzz Sound

Park-branded Sola Sound fuzz. Early examples use the Tone Bender MKIII family and date to 1969. Fuzzboxes says the model moved to the pressed-steel enclosure used for Tone Bender MKIV-type production in the early 1970s and continued to the mid-1970s.

Sources:
- https://fuzzboxes.org/parkfuzzsound
- https://fuzzboxes.org/timeline

### Sola Sound Tone Bender MKIV

Appears around 1970. Early examples have the later-informal “bendy”/“spaghetti” graphic style; by approximately 1971 a recognizable “Batman” logo/artwork version appears and the MKIV designation is removed from the enclosure graphics.

Archive rule: “bendy/spaghetti” and “Batman” are collector/research descriptors unless primary documentation demonstrates official naming.

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/tonebendermkiv

## Japanese OEM discovery

### Teisco Fuzz Machine

Effects Database describes a distinctive Japanese Fuzz Machine sold under multiple brand labels, including Ibanez Standard Fuzz model 59, Bruno Fuzz Machine, Mica Fuzz, Marlboro Wailer Fuzz, Antoria Fuzz Machine and Aria Diamond Fuzz. The page also connects the design to Shin-Ei/Teisco and an FY-6/Super-Fuzz-related family.

Archive priority: P1

Critical model:
**OEM family > retail label > model number > individual specimen**

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/teisco/fuzzmachine

### Teisco TF-1 Fuzz

Effects Database describes this as another Japanese FY-6 Super Fuzz family product, sold under the Teisco name.

Source: https://www.effectsdatabase.com/model/teisco/tf1

## Fuzzrite / Mosrite / Rosac branch

Fuzzboxes' Fuzzrite history identifies later silicon-transistor Fuzzrite-derived products and Rosac Nu-Fuzz family products, including colorful 1970s examples, eyelet-board and prefabricated-module construction, and the Rosac Distortion Blender.

It also documents a short-lived Don Wehr product that was a rebranded Rosac Nu-Fuzz, illustrating another OEM/rebranding path.

Archive research needed:
- original Mosrite Fuzzrite
- brown/pink/red/blue/other production periods
- Japanese Rosac/Nu-Fuzz-related products
- Don Wehr rebrand
- Distortion Blender
- modern reproductions

Source: https://fuzzboxes.org/features/fuzzrite

## Important database implications from vintage research

### 1. Manufacturer and builder are separate

A product branded “Marshall” may have been built by Sola Sound during one production period and by Marshall during another.

### 2. Brand and family are separate

The Vox Tone Bender name can refer to British Sola Sound production or other Vox-branded products made elsewhere.

### 3. A model can outlive a manufacturing relationship

The customer-facing name may stay constant while the actual manufacturer changes.

### 4. A production change is not automatically a new model

Graphic, knob, enclosure or component changes should normally become distinguishers or production-period records unless the evidence supports a new generation.

### 5. Sparse evidence is still valuable

Sperrin and Kelly should remain in the archive even without surviving specimens. Their pages should simply state that the surviving evidence is extremely limited.

### 6. Collector terminology must be labeled

Terms like “MK1.5,” “Batman,” “spaghetti graphics” and researcher-specific Big Muff version numbers should be explicitly identified as archival/collector terminology when they are not builder terminology.
