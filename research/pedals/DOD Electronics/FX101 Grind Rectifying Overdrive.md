## What this pedal is
The **DOD FX101 Grind Rectifying Overdrive** is a late-1990s DOD dirt pedal designed to create a modern, high-gain **rectifier-style** sound. DOD's manual describes a special circuit that adds even harmonics to emulate an overdriven tube response and then rectifies the signal to produce what the manufacturer calls a modern “rectifier crunch” sound. [1][2]

Although DOD classified it as an **overdrive**, the documented gain structure and contemporary descriptions place it toward high-gain distortion territory. The archive keeps the catalog taxonomy as Overdrive because that is the canonical record identity while preserving the manufacturer's own model name and description. [1][2]

## Controls and signal shaping
The FX101 has four principal controls:
- **Blast:** output level.
- **Rumble:** low-frequency EQ.
- **Burn:** high-frequency EQ.
- **Fear:** gain / overdrive amount. [1]

DOD's manual explains that Rumble and Burn are centered around common amplifier-style frequency areas. The company positioned the result as behaving more like a preamp than a conventional pedal, with enough tonal shaping to move from hard rock and alternative sounds toward more extreme grind-oriented tones. [1]

The pedal also has a dedicated **SPKR OUT** jack with speaker emulation. DOD presented this as a direct connection for a mixing board or recording system, removing the need for a separate direct box in the intended setup. [1][2]

## Historical introduction and production
Independent exact-model references place the FX101's introduction at **Summer NAMM 1996**, alongside the DOD FX100 Integrated Tube Even Harmonic Overdrive. The FX101 and FX100 shared a circuit-board platform, with changes used to produce their different voicings. [3]

Those same sources report a production run from **1996 through 1998**, with the model discontinued by late 1998 as DOD revised its effects lineup. This is historical production information from secondary exact-model research, not a current manufacturer statement. [3]

DOD's current manual index still lists an FX101 Grind manual, preserving the model's support documentation even though the pedal is not presented there as a current-production product. [4]

## Circuit and technical evidence
Secondary circuit research reports that the FX101 shares the same basic board as the **FX100 Integrated Tube Even Harmonic Overdrive**, but with several component changes and omissions that distinguish the Grind voicing. [3]

The same technical source reports **two 4560-type op-amps** in the FX101 and identifies the FX100 as its closest circuit relation. A separate technical reference also lists the FX101 among DOD's I.T. design family. These are secondary component-level observations, so the archive does not treat them as a universal BOM for every surviving FX101 without unit-specific verification. [3]

The documented design concept is more important than a speculative “tube” claim: DOD describes added even harmonics as an electronic emulation of overdriven tube behavior. The pedal contains no actual vacuum tube in the reviewed documentation. [1][3]

## Controls and operating behavior
DOD's operating guidance emphasizes establishing the desired output with Blast, then using Fear to set the desired amount of grind, with Rumble and Burn shaping the low and high ends. The SPKR OUT can be used when feeding a board or recorder directly. [1]

Contemporary user reports describe the Rumble and Burn controls as intentionally broad and amplifier-like, with high Burn settings producing a pronounced treble character and Rumble providing substantial low-frequency weight. Those are listening observations rather than laboratory frequency-response measurements. [5]

## Relationship to FX100
The FX101 should remain separate from the **FX100 Integrated Tube Even Harmonic Overdrive** in the archive:
- **FX100:** DOD's “Integrated Tube Even Harmonic Overdrive.”
- **FX101:** DOD's “Grind Rectifying Overdrive.”
- Both were introduced in the same 1996 period.
- Secondary board analysis identifies a shared circuit-board foundation with model-specific component changes. [3]

This relationship is useful archival context, but it does not mean the two pedals are interchangeable or electronically identical.
