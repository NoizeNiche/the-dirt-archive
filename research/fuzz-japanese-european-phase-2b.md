# The Dirt Archive — Japanese & European Fuzz Research, Phase 2B

This phase continues the Japanese and European under-documented fuzz survey. It prioritizes family relationships, branded/OEM identities, production windows, and external identification clues. It deliberately avoids publishing complete circuit recipes, schematics, PCB layouts, or cloning instructions.

## ELK: Big Muff / Super Fuzz Sustainar branch

### ELK Super Fuzz Sustainar / ES-960
ELK's own current historical material identifies the company as founded in 1966 and describes the historical Super Fuzz Sustainar as a product of the 1970s era. ELK states that the historical unit was a fuzz whose character leaned toward distortion, with pronounced low-frequency weight.

A 2026 Tone Machines Blog archival investigation reports that the earliest catalog appearance the researcher located for the ELK Super Fuzz Sustainar was the 1973 ELK catalog. Treat 1973 as an evidence-backed earliest catalog sighting rather than an absolute manufacturing-start date until additional advertisements or catalog sheets are found.

Effects Database treats the ELK Big Muff Sustainar as a Japanese Big Muff interpretation and records later ES-960 Super Fuzz Sustainar and other related forms. Because historical sources disagree on exact manufacturer relationships for some examples, the archive should maintain separate records for Electro Sound, ELK-branded, and unbranded examples while relating them only when the evidence supports a common production lineage.

### Why this family matters
The ELK branch is historically important because it sits at the intersection of Japanese OEM production, Big Muff interpretation, brand changes, and later modern reinterpretations. Modern builders copying an ELK unit should be related as descendants/reinterpretations, not merged into the ELK manufacturer record.

## ELK Wau Fuzz / Wau & Sustainer
Effects Database identifies the ELK WF-1100 Wau Fuzz and WS-1400 Wau & Sustainer as distinct historical products. They belong in the Japanese OEM research network alongside the Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz and Guyatone FS-5, but should not automatically be treated as identical merely because their enclosures or fuzz/wah concepts are related.

## Pearl CF-1 Cry Fuzz
The Pearl CF-1 Cry Fuzz is a Japanese fuzz-wah pedal generally placed in the 1970s. The unit is valuable to the archive because it combines a treadle wah with a fuzz section and has a distinctive visual identity. A 2026 Tone Machines Blog investigation notes evidence that the CF-1's fuzz section is closely related to the Japanese Big Muff family and discusses similarities to ELK products. This is a promising research connection, but the archive should label the exact manufacturer/lineage relationship as Probable until stronger primary documentation is found.

## Guyatone FS series
Effects Database currently lists eight FS-series products, including:

- FS-1 Bazz Box
- FS-1 Buzz Box
- FS-2 Buzz Box
- FS-3 Fuzz
- FS-5 Wah-Fuzz
- FS-6 Sustainer
- FS-4 Wah-Wah (important as the non-fuzz member of the same historical pedal family)
- FS-7 Leslie Queen / Rolly Queen

The archive should retain the entire FS family as a Guyatone product family while linking only the relevant dirt products into fuzz/wah research.

### FS-1 naming problem
Guyatone used both Bazz Box and Buzz Box naming within the FS-1 designation. This is exactly the sort of naming collision that can cause false “generation” records. Record the names as separate branded/model entries with a relationship note, then investigate control layouts, enclosure graphics, catalogs, and internal evidence before deciding whether they represent revisions, regional naming, or separate products.

### FS-3 Fuzz
Earlier research already identified FS-3 as a probable Super-Fuzz-related OEM product, including a reported Honey-labeled PCB relationship. Preserve that relationship as Probable, not Confirmed, until more independent evidence is found.

### FS-5 Wah-Fuzz
FS-5 belongs to the Japanese OEM fuzz-wah network also represented by Teisco and other labels. It is a useful anchor record for studying how the same mechanical treadle platform and fuzz/wah architecture traveled between retail brands.

## Teisco Fuzz Machine
Effects Database identifies the Teisco Fuzz Machine as an early-1970s Japanese pedal distributed under numerous labels, including Ibanez Standard Fuzz model 59, Bruno, Mica, Marlboro Wailer, Antoria, and Aria Diamond. The distinctive dual-slider/twin-switch enclosure is a strong external identification clue.

Because this product is frequently discussed online through circuit-family comparisons, the public archive should emphasize the enclosure, controls, branding, labels, and OEM relationships rather than publish a reconstruction of the circuit.

## Teisco TF-1
The Teisco TF-1 is documented by Effects Database as a Teisco-branded FY-6-family product. It should remain a separate model record from the Teisco Fuzz Machine even though both are Japanese fuzzes associated with similar historical markets.

## Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz
The Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz belongs to a larger early/mid-1970s Japanese OEM fuzz-wah network. Effects Database relates it to the Ibanez Wau Wau Fuzz, Guyatone FS-5, Elk Wau Fuzz, Mica, Antoria, Lord, Aria Diamond, Bruno, and Memphis forms. This network should be modeled as a family/relationship graph rather than a flat list of unrelated pedals.

## Kurosawa / Japanese "The Fuzz" candidate
Effects Database documents a rare Japanese pedal attributed to Kurosawa Musical Instruments, generally dated to the late 1970s or 1980s, with a Super-Fuzz-derived family relationship. This should be treated as an archival candidate rather than a high-confidence mainstream model because the surviving documentation is sparse.

Useful external identifiers include the compact Japanese enclosure, base marking, unusual control arrangement, and two-mode behavior. Any exact internal implementation should remain out of the public archive except where a component distinction is historically necessary for identification.

## Ace Tone FM family
Ace Tone's FM-1, FM-2, and FM-3 should be treated as three separate model records.

A 2025 Tone Machines Blog deep-dive on the FM-2 found a period advertisement from March 1968 and catalog evidence continuing into at least 1979. The researcher also reported that hundreds of examined examples showed little meaningful exterior cosmetic change. This makes specimen-level evidence especially important for chronology.

Archive treatment:
- earliest documented ad date = 1968-03, Confirmed as an advertisement sighting by the cited researcher
- catalog presence through 1979 = Reported/Strong lead, not an absolute production end
- exact production period = Open / specimen-dependent
- exterior variation = apparently limited, so serials, labels, date marks, and ownership provenance may matter more than paint/graphics

## Maxon / Nisshin Onpa: OEM research priority
Nisshin Onpa established itself as an OEM manufacturer during the 1960s and began producing effects by 1969. Maxon became its major branded effects identity while OEM relationships also supplied other companies, including Ibanez.

For The Dirt Archive, this means Maxon should be researched in two dimensions:

1. Maxon-branded dirt products.
2. OEM products built by Nisshin Onpa but sold under another retail brand.

Do not assume an Ibanez product is automatically an "Ibanez builder" record. The archive's builder/brand separation is particularly important here.

The Tube Screamer history is outside this fuzz phase's immediate priority, but the same Nisshin Onpa OEM methodology should eventually be applied to early fuzz-wah and fuzz products.

## Selmer Fuzz-Wah
The Vintage Hofner/UK historical archive documents a Selmer Fuzz-Wah first version from 1968 to approximately 1971. Designer John Weir reported that Selmer asked him in 1968 to create a pedal combining fuzz, wah-wah, and a repeat/chop effect, using two press-operated switches. The same source documents a second version beginning in 1971 and suggests that later examples may have been imported from the Far East.

This is unusually valuable evidence because the source ties chronology to a named designer's testimony and also documents a transition from local design/production to imported equipment.

Archive treatment:
- Version 1: 1968–c.1971, Confirmed by period archive/testimony
- Version 2: from 1971, exact endpoint open
- production-source transition: Reported by designer testimony; preserve wording rather than overstate certainty
- mechanical controls are an important identification distinguisher

## Italian / JEN / Vox branch
Secondary Italian historical research continues to support a JEN-manufactured Vox V828/V8281 Tone Bender branch, with evidence suggesting an earlier EKO connection but no definitive confirmation of the exact handoff point. One surviving example cited by the researcher bears a 13 November 1967 construction date.

Archive treatment:
- Vox V828/V8281 = separate retail model
- JEN = manufacturer relationship
- EKO involvement = Disputed
- 13 Nov 1967 date = specimen evidence, not automatically a universal start date
- later Elka/Luxor/Nova/Unicord naming should be modeled as brand/distribution relationships where documented

## Preservation rule: distinguish "same circuit family" from "same product"
The current research repeatedly demonstrates why the archive cannot collapse products merely because collector sources use phrases such as "clone," "copy," "based on," "same circuit," or "Super Fuzz type."

The archive should distinguish:

- same product sold under multiple labels
- same OEM family sold under multiple brands
- later redesign using a related topology
- direct clone/reissue
- modern interpretation
- merely similar sonic behavior

These are different historical relationships and should be encoded differently in the database.
