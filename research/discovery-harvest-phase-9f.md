# The Dirt Archive — Discovery Harvest Phase 9F

## Focus

This pass follows the Japanese and U.S. obscurities from 9E into several European and Commonwealth records. The emphasis is on builders and branded products that are historically useful but easy to overlook in a famous-pedal-first catalog.

## 1. Jansen Fuzzman

**Builder / designer:** Jansen / Bruce Eady
**Country:** New Zealand
**Model:** Fuzzman
**Period:** mid-1960s to early 1970s, according to Effects Database

Effects Database describes the Fuzzman as a locally produced New Zealand fuzz with limited export distribution. Two enclosure styles are documented: an earlier rectangular project-box form and a later tooled metal case. The record also distinguishes an earlier germanium period from later silicon examples.

### Archive value
This is exactly the kind of regional survival the discovery layer should retain. It documents that the early fuzz boom was not simply a U.K./U.S./Japan story. A later production-period record can separate the enclosure transition and device-family transition without publishing circuit reconstruction information.

### Public-facing treatment
- Preserve both enclosure forms as production-period distinguishers.
- Preserve germanium/silicon transition as a high-level technical distinguisher because it materially identifies period.
- Do not publish a schematic or full parts list.
- Keep Bruce Eady as a designer attribution rather than automatically treating every Jansen-branded unit as a personally built one-off.

## 2. Herald Electronics AM-44A Fuzz Master

**Builder / manufacturer:** Herald Electronics
**Country:** Japan
**Model:** AM-44A Fuzz Master
**Period:** late 1960s
**Branding network:** Apollo, LRE, Melos, Mica, Monacor, Olson, according to EDB

This is a high-value Japanese OEM/rebrand record. Effects Database identifies the AM-44A as a Japanese-made solid-state fuzz distributed under multiple regional brand names and links it to the LRE Fuzz Sound family.

### Archive treatment
Create one manufacturer/model record for Herald AM-44A and separate brand records for the rebrand names only when evidence supports the relationship. Do not collapse every badge into a single model name. This directly exercises the archive's rule that a consumer-facing brand can differ from its manufacturer.

## 3. EF-EL / Calderoni Musica Italian Vox fuzz lineage

**Manufacturer:** EF-EL / Calderoni Musica
**Country:** Italy
**Model:** Vox (by EF-EL) Distortion
**Approx. period:** circa 1969–1971

Despite its period-facing "Distortion" name, EDB identifies the product as a fuzz and places it within an Italian manufacturing family that also includes Cosmosound, G.I.S., and Silversound products. The record is particularly useful because the Vox badge can otherwise lead researchers to assume a British origin.

### Archive treatment
Record:
- Vox (by EF-EL) Distortion as the branded model
- EF-EL / Calderoni Musica as manufacturer attribution
- Italian origin
- Approximate 1969–71 period
- Related-product relationships to the documented EF-EL family

Avoid publishing the circuit topology details present on the EDB page. The important archival fact is the Italian manufacturing relationship and the model's place in the early European fuzz market.

## 4. Moreschi Octave Fuzz

**Manufacturer:** Cav. Giovanni Moreschi
**Country:** Italy
**Model:** Octave Fuzz
**Period:** 1970s, with EDB giving a mid-1970s origin
**Designer relationship:** Gary Hurst / Electronic Sounds collaboration according to EDB

Moreschi is historically valuable because the maker is better known for accordions than guitar effects. The Octave Fuzz belongs to a short-lived Italian effects program and is linked by EDB to the Electronic Sounds UFO Octa-Fuzz and CBS-Arbiter Doubler family.

### Archive treatment
Preserve:
- Moreschi as manufacturer
- Gary Hurst / Electronic Sounds as design relationship, marked according to evidence strength
- Octave Fuzz as a model
- metal enclosure and octave-function distinction
- family relationship to the other documented Hurst-associated octave products

Do not carry over EDB's detailed circuit claims into the public archive.

## 5. Electronic Sounds UFO Octa-Fuzz

**Brand / design entity:** Electronic Sounds plastic UFO series
**Designer attribution:** Gary Hurst, according to EDB
**Country:** Italy
**Period:** early–mid 1970s

The UFO Octa-Fuzz is an important Hurst post-Tone-Bender lead because it provides a visible bridge from the famous British Tone Bender lineage into later Italian work. EDB relates the model to the CBS-Arbiter Doubler and Moreschi Octave Fuzz.

### Archive treatment
Keep this as its own model, not a clone or footnote to Tone Bender. The historical interest is the career/design lineage and the Italian manufacturing period. Record the UFO enclosure/series distinction as a physical identifier.

## 6. Pigdog Fuzz 1.5

**Builder:** Pigdog
**Country:** UK
**Builder type:** one-person boutique workshop
**Model:** Fuzz 1.5
**Period:** modern; EDB publication 2010

The Pigdog record is useful as a boutique-preservation model because the builder explicitly documented period-style materials and construction choices, including 1960s NOS board material and vintage component families. It was hand-made in Surrey, London.

The archive should not turn those details into a parts recipe. Instead, preserve the historically meaningful high-level statement that the builder intentionally used vintage-spec/period-origin materials and that the Fuzz 1.5 represents the builder's interpretation of 1960s fuzz construction.

## 7. Kurosawa / Japanese unknown family follow-up

The current Effects Database fuzz index still exposes multiple unresolved Japanese records such as Big Mag Sustainar, Fuzzie and Crazy Face/Crazy Tone. The safest archival approach is to preserve each as an individual discovery record and connect them with a **provisional related-to relationship** rather than prematurely assigning one manufacturer.

The Kurosawa The Fuzz record is stronger because a surviving specimen reportedly carries Kurosawa and PRAT-7 board markings. That evidence supports a "provisional attribution" state, not a definitive corporate manufacturing claim. citeturn997074search0turn997074search12

## 8. Discovery philosophy update

Regional records such as Jansen and Moreschi demonstrate why builder-first browsing is the right architecture. A user may arrive searching for a familiar name, but the archive's historical value often emerges when that name is revealed to have been:

- a local manufacturer,
- a retail/store brand,
- an OEM badge,
- a designer collaboration,
- or a later regional reinterpretation.

The database should preserve those roles independently so the eventual lineage graph can answer questions the original marketplace listings cannot.
