# The Dirt Archive — Japanese & European Fuzz Families, Phase 2C

Research pass focused on Japanese OEM networks, early fuzz-wah hybrids, obscure branded variants, and European/American edge cases. This is a research ledger, not automatic public-archive approval.

## Evidence discipline

The most useful discoveries in this area are relationship clues rather than isolated model facts. Brand names can represent retailers, importers, contract manufacturers, or alternate labels. The archive should preserve those distinctions.

Dates in this file are treated as approximate unless supported by a period advertisement, catalog, dated documentation, surviving specimen evidence, or a strong first-party statement. Technical information is limited to identification-relevant distinctions. No schematics, PCB diagrams, full bills of materials, or cloning instructions are included.

---

## 1. The Japanese fuzz-wah OEM network deserves its own family layer

The Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz and related records reveal a particularly dense OEM network. Effects Database connects the family through examples branded as Bruno, Aria Diamond, Electra, Elk, Excetro, Guyatone, Ibanez, Lord, Luxor, Mica, Pax, Selmer and Teisco. Several records explicitly identify PCB/model relationships, while some builder attributions remain probabilistic.

This is more useful for the archive than treating each logo as a separate invention.

### Strong relationship cluster

- Bruno Wau Wau Fuzz
- Aria Diamond WFP-1 Wah-Fuzz
- Electra model 1244 Wah Fuzz
- Elk WF-1100 Wau Fuzz
- Excetro Wau Wau Fuzz
- Guyatone FS-5 Wah-Fuzz
- Ibanez 58 Standard Wau Fuzz
- Lord Wah-Fuzz
- Luxor Wah-Fuzz, attributed by Effects Database to Maxon
- Mica Wau Wau Fuzz
- Mica Wau Wau Wailer
- Pax Pedal Number One
- Selmer Wau Wau Fuzz
- Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz

Effects Database notes the WFP-1 PCB designation on a number of these variants and separately identifies the Maxon attribution on the Luxor version. The archive should preserve these as relationships, not merge every record into a single "model." [Source: Effects Database, Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz and Selmer Wau Wau Fuzz related records.]

### Archive treatment

Recommended hierarchy:

**OEM family:** Japanese Wau-Wau Fuzz / WFP-1 family (research name)
→ **retail / brand model:** individual branded pedals
→ **production period:** specimen-supported where possible
→ **technical distinguisher:** enclosure, label, control graphics, PCB family only when needed for identification

The name "WFP-1 family" is useful as an internal research handle when directly supported by surviving model/PCB relationships. It should not automatically be presented as the original manufacturer's official family name.

## 2. Teisco Fuzz Machine versus FY-6-derived Teisco TF-1

These must remain separate.

### Teisco Fuzz Machine

Effects Database identifies this as an early-1970s Japanese fuzz distributed under numerous names, including Ibanez Standard Fuzz model No. 59, Bruno Fuzz Machine, Mica Fuzz, Marlboro Wailer Fuzz, Antoria Fuzz Machine and Aria Diamond Fuzz. Its characteristic external identification features are the dual-slider controls and twin-switch enclosure.

The same source describes this design as a Shin-Ei-developed design manufactured through Teisco/OEM channels and related to the Univox Super-Fuzz/FY-6 lineage. It should therefore be represented as a distinct family node rather than simply as another FY-6 rebrand.

**Evidence state:** family relationship = strong secondary-source evidence; exact manufacturing-chain details = retain as documented attribution, not absolute ownership claim.

### Teisco TF-1 Fuzz

Effects Database identifies the TF-1 as another Japanese product associated with the FY-6 Super-Fuzz family, with a simpler external control scheme.

**Critical archive rule:** do not collapse TF-1 and the Teisco Fuzz Machine just because both belong to the broad Shin-Ei/Teisco vintage fuzz ecosystem.

## 3. The Big Muff copy family in Japan is now demonstrably richer than a single Elk entry

Current research shows a network that includes:

- Elk Big Muff Sustainar
- Electro Sound Big Muff Sustainer
- Elk Gakki Co. / New Big Muff Sustainar
- unbranded Big Muff Sustainar
- Maccanbell Big Muff Sustainar
- This Sounds Better Big Mag
- Sekova SE-2015 Big Muff
- Big Mag / related unidentified Japanese variants
- Guyatone FS-6 SustainER
- Aria Distortion SustainER
- Aria Super Fuzz SustainER

The June 2026 Tone Machines Blog investigation is especially important because it uses period Elk catalogs and magazine advertisements to establish an early-1970s sequence rather than relying on collector repetition.

### Elk chronology lead

The research finds the earliest known appearance of the Elk Super Fuzz Sustainar in a **1973 Elk catalog**, where it is listed as the **ES-960 Super Fuzz Sustainer**. The same investigation says the following year's material shows the WF-1100 Wau-Fuzz and WS-1400 Wau & Sustainer as well.

The 1973–75 evidence is valuable because it anchors the product in period documentation. Tone Machines Blog reports that the Electro Sound Big Muff version is also an Elk product, despite separate branding, addresses, and advertisements.

The investigation further reports an evolution in nomenclature from **SustainER** to **SustainAR**, with the transition appearing in period material. This is an excellent example of a small cosmetic/label detail becoming a legitimate production-period distinguisher.

Later Elk versions used a revised PCB and changed transistor families, among other internal changes. For the public archive, record the existence of meaningful production changes and identify period boundaries only when enough external/specimen evidence exists; do not publish component recipes.

**Evidence state:** 1973 catalog appearance = strong period evidence. Detailed version chronology = research-grade but still subject to revision as more catalogs and specimens are examined.

### Important corrective

The old collector shorthand that these Elk products were simply "Japanese Big Muff copies from 1970" is too coarse. The period documentation now supports a later starting point for at least the documented Elk Super Fuzz Sustainar line.

Source: Tone Machines Blog, *History of the Elk Big Muff Sustainar*, June 11, 2026.

## 4. Guyatone FS-6 SustainER is a major preservation candidate

The Guyatone FS-6 deserves an individual archive entry rather than being buried in the general Guyatone FS-series list.

Tone Machines Blog's surviving-catalog research dates the FS-6 to approximately **1974–1975**, describing it as appearing in both the 1974 and 1975 Guyatone catalogs. The researcher identifies it as an unusual Japanese early-1970s fuzz with a Big Muff-like architecture and some other vintage-fuzz influences.

The most important archival point is not its modern rarity or resale value. It is that this appears to be a short production run represented in period company catalogs, making catalog capture and specimen photography valuable historical preservation.

**Recommended record:**
- Builder/entity: Guyatone / Japanese manufacturer history TBD in database
- Model: FS-6 SustainER
- Family: Japanese Big Muff-influenced fuzz (research family)
- Production: circa 1974–75, provisional until more period evidence is found
- Evidence: catalog appearances + surviving specimen documentation

Source: Tone Machines Blog, *Guyatone Sustainer FS-6 (1974)*.

## 5. Pearl CF-1 Cry Fuzz: do not overstate the lineage

Effects Database identifies the Pearl CF-1 as a Japanese fuzz-wah pedal. Contemporary listings describe it as a 1970s Japanese unit.

A June 2026 Tone Machines Blog investigation found evidence that the CF-1 contains a four-transistor fuzz section with two sets of clipping diodes and similarities to the Japanese Big Muff/Elk ecosystem. The same investigation notes a particularly unusual connection between the CF-1's clipping components and those found in an unbranded Japanese Big Muff Sustainar.

This is **interesting evidence, not proof of direct manufacture by Elk**. The archive should therefore use language such as:

- "shows technical similarities to the Japanese Big Muff/Elk ecosystem"
- "possible shared component/supplier relationship"
- "manufacturer lineage unresolved"

Do not promote the CF-1 to an "Elk-made Pearl" record without stronger evidence.

**Evidence state:** Japanese 1970s fuzz-wah = strong general evidence; direct Elk manufacturing relationship = unverified / speculative.

Sources: Effects Database Pearl CF-1; Tone Machines Blog, *History of the Elk Big Muff Sustainar*, June 2026.

## 6. Selmer fuzz-wah products belong in both British and OEM research

Selmer's effects lineup includes both a Fuzz-Wah and Wau Wau Fuzz-related products. Effects Database preserves these as separate records and shows their links into the broader Japanese OEM Wau-Wau network.

The Selmer Wau Wau Fuzz record is particularly useful because its related-product graph connects it to the same group of Japanese branded variants as Teisco, Guyatone, Ibanez, Aria Diamond, Lord, Mica and others.

A separate Vintage Hofner/Selmer research source records a Selmer Fuzz-Wah development beginning around **1968**, including a later version from approximately 1971. This gives the archive a potential British product-development timeline that should not automatically be conflated with the later Japanese OEM-branded units.

**Archive rule:**

"Selmer Fuzz-Wah" and "Selmer Wau Wau Fuzz" are presently separate model records. Their relationship, if any, needs direct evidence rather than name similarity.

Sources: Effects Database Selmer Fuzz-Wah; Effects Database Selmer Wau Wau Fuzz; Vintage Hofner/Selmer history.

## 7. Maxon / Nisshin Onpa is a manufacturer-level research priority

Maxon is not merely a later Tube Screamer story. Nisshin Onpa began as a Japanese OEM/component manufacturer and expanded into effects production during the late 1960s. The company's effects were sold under multiple trade names, including Ibanez.

The archive should therefore maintain a manufacturer/entity record for **Nisshin Onpa / Maxon** that can own multiple model and OEM relationships rather than attaching all Maxon-associated products directly to a single consumer-facing brand.

The Japanese Wau-Wau Fuzz graph contains a particularly useful lead: Effects Database identifies the **Luxor Wah-Fuzz** as "by Maxon" and associates it with the WFP-1 PCB family. Meanwhile the Elk WF-1100 Wau Fuzz is also associated with a Maxon PCB in Effects Database's relationship graph.

That does not prove that Maxon manufactured every product in the Wau-Wau network. It does strongly justify a research branch dedicated to Maxon's OEM and subcontracting relationships.

**Next evidence target:** period Maxon/Ibanez catalogs, Japanese trade ads, importer documentation and specimen photographs showing shared enclosure/board identifiers.

Sources: Maxon historical references; Effects Database Wau-Wau Fuzz related records.

## 8. Suzuki / Takeuchi / SonaTone: obscure non-FY branches

Effects Database documents a **Suzuki Fuzz-Tone / Distorsion** sold in Mexico in the 1970s with Japanese parts. A contributor identifies it as a Shin-Ei OEM product but specifically argues that it is **not** an FY-series Super-Fuzz-family design. Instead, it appears to be a Japanese clone of the Maestro FZ-1 concept.

This is precisely the kind of record the archive should preserve while keeping the evidence label visible:

**Suzuki Fuzz-Tone / Distorsion**
- Era: 1970s, provisional
- Market context: sold in Mexico
- Construction: Japanese parts are documented by surviving-specimen report
- Proposed builder lineage: Shin-Ei OEM, **reported / unverified**
- Design lineage: Maestro FZ-1-style, **reported / requires stronger corroboration**

Effects Database's relationship graph also connects the mystery pedal to **Takeuchi Fuzz-Tone / Distorsion** and **SonaTone Fuzz / Distorsion**. These are excellent candidates for specimen-led research because they could document an independent Japanese export/OEM pathway that would otherwise disappear.

Source: Effects Database Suzuki Fuzz-Tone / Distorsion.

## 9. Maestro FZ-1 family remains foundational and should be split by generation

The archive should not use a single "Maestro FZ-1" entry for all historical descendants.

Recommended records:

- FZ-1
- FZ-1A
- FZ-1B
- FZ-1S Super-Fuzz

The FZ-1 and FZ-1A are the germanium-era products. Later versions represent more substantial circuit and packaging changes. Contemporary historical sources identify the FZ-1 as the first commercially successful mass-market fuzz design, introduced in 1962, and Gibson/industry histories link its later evolution to the mid-1960s fuzz explosion.

Effects Database also preserves shipment figures by year, while historical technical research distinguishes the FZ-1/FZ-1A from the later silicon-based versions.

For the Dirt Archive, the most useful public distinctions are:

- enclosure shape
- branding / label wording
- battery arrangement
- control labeling
- germanium versus silicon generation
- manufacturer-location transitions where independently documented
- later FZ-1S as a distinct design generation, not merely a cosmetic revision

Sources: Effects Database Maestro; Guitar World Maestro history; BOSS historical fuzz article; historical technical study by David Morrin.

## 11. Early Ross / American production should be kept separate from Japanese OEM fuzz

Ross is worth preserving primarily as a later American builder lineage, rather than forcing it into the vintage-Japanese chapter.

Effects Database describes Ross as a Keas Electronics brand associated with Bud Ross and the Kustom manufacturing ecosystem. Ross entered the pedal market in the late 1970s. Early enclosures visually resembled MXR-style housings before a proprietary angled enclosure appeared following trade-dress pressure.

The newer JHS-era Ross Fuzz is historically interesting for a different reason: Effects Database explicitly notes that no original Ross Fuzz existed in the old Ross catalog and that the newer pedal draws its fuzz concept from Kustom amplifier history.

**Archive rule:** the JHS Ross Fuzz should be classified as a modern revival/interpretation, not backfilled into the original Ross historical catalog.

Sources: Effects Database Ross; Effects Database Ross Fuzz by JHS.
