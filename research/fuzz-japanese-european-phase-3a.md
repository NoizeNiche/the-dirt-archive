# The Dirt Archive — Japanese / European / OEM Fuzz Discovery, Phase 3A

This is a discovery-stage research ledger. It is intentionally broader than the verified public archive. Records below are candidates for normalization and later promotion. Exact dates, OEM attribution, and circuit lineage are not treated as settled merely because a secondary database groups them together.

## 1. Japan: expanding the Maxon / Nisshin Onpa branch

### Ibanez first-series dirt records
Effects Database identifies the Ibanez first-series family as largely made by Maxon, with 32 products. Dirt-relevant records include:
- Ibanez 57 Standard Wau Wau
- Ibanez 58 Standard Wau Fuzz
- Ibanez 59 Standard Fuzz
- Ibanez 83 Fuzz
- Ibanez 84 Double Sound
- Ibanez 85 Bass Booster
- Ibanez 86 Power Booster
- Ibanez 87 Treble Booster
- Ibanez 88 Distortion Booster
- Ibanez Distortion Sustainer
- Ibanez OD-850 Overdrive
- Ibanez OD-855 Overdrive II

The first-series catalog structure is valuable because it lets the archive distinguish a retail-brand product from the Japanese manufacturer behind many of the units.

### Ibanez 59 Standard Fuzz / Teisco Fuzz Machine relationship
The Ibanez 59 Standard Fuzz is separately catalogued by Effects Database, while the Teisco Fuzz Machine entry identifies the same distinctive early-1970s enclosure family as having been sold under the Ibanez Standard Fuzz No. 59 name. Treat this as a high-priority relationship to reconcile rather than duplicate as unrelated designs.

### Greco
Discovery records include:
- Greco SD-100 Fuzz
- Greco No. 35 Haztone / Fuzz Booster

The Greco No. 35 entry is especially useful because recent research clarifies the Romanization issue around the Japanese product name (Hazutone/Haztone variants in English-language sources) and places the unit in the same broad Japanese wedge-enclosure field as Sekova and other early imports. OEM manufacturer attribution remains a candidate rather than a confirmed builder identity.

### Sekova Model 59 Distortion Box
Effects Database identifies the Sekova Model 59 as a rare Japanese-made late-1960s export fuzz. Secondary evidence points toward Nisshin Onpa / the later Maxon organization, with construction similarities to Greco, LRE and early Ibanez products. Archive treatment: model confirmed as a Sekova-branded Japanese fuzz; manufacturer relationship = probable, not definitive.

### Guyatone
Current Effects Database discovery yields a substantial historical dirt target set:
- FS-1 Bazz Box
- FS-1 Buzz Box
- FS-2 Buzz Box
- FS-3 Fuzz
- FS-5 Wah-Fuzz
- FS-6 Sustainer / Fuzz Sustainer
- FZ-2000 Fuzz
- TZ2 The Fuzz
- TZm5 Torrid Fuzz

The two FS-1 names must remain separate records until catalog chronology establishes whether this is a naming collision, regional naming variation, or separate hardware.

FS-3 is reported in Effects Database as a 1972–73 product and a Honey-labeled PCB relationship. Treat the Honey/OEM inference as probable pending primary-source confirmation.

FS-6 deserves separate archival attention because period Guyatone catalogs are reported for 1974 and 1975, making it a useful post-FS-3 chronology marker.

TZ2 is a later Super-Fuzz-influenced Guyatone design and should not be merged with FY-6 OEM products merely because the sonic family resemblance is strong.

TZm5 Torrid Fuzz is a later Guyatone fuzz associated with engineer Toshi Torii. Preserve its later-generation identity separately from the 1960s/70s Honey/Shin-Ei lineage.

### Teisco
Discovery targets:
- Teisco Fuzz
- Teisco Fuzz Machine
- Teisco TF-1 Fuzz
- Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz

The Teisco Fuzz Machine is an especially important OEM candidate because it appears under multiple retail names, including Ibanez Standard Fuzz No. 59, Bruno Fuzz Machine, Mica Fuzz, Marlboro Wailer Fuzz, Antoria Fuzz Machine and Aria Diamond Fuzz.

Teisco TF-1 must remain distinct from Teisco Fuzz Machine despite both belonging to Japanese vintage fuzz history. Effects Database treats TF-1 as an FY-6/Super Fuzz family rebrand.

Teisco Wau Wau Fuzz belongs to a different fuzz-wah OEM network associated with Ibanez Wau Wau Fuzz, Guyatone FS-5, Elk WF-1100 and other regional names. This network is a priority for relationship normalization.

### Elk
Current discovery list:
- Elk Big Muff Sustainar
- Elk ES-960 Super Fuzz Sustainar
- Elk SFS-1 Super Fuzz Sustainar reissue
- Elk WF-1100 Wau Fuzz
- Elk WS-1400 Wau & Sustainer

Elk's dirt products should be modeled as a family cluster rather than one generic “Elk fuzz.” The Big Muff Sustainar and Super Fuzz Sustainar occupy different historical lineages even where collector discussions sometimes blur them.

### Aria / Aria Diamond
Current Effects Database Aria index includes:
- Aria FW-1 Fuzz Wah Machine
- Aria RP-2 Fuzz Wah Wah
- Aria Cruncher
- Aria TD-1 Platinum Drive

The Aria Diamond-branded examples also appear in Japanese OEM networks, especially the Teisco/SHIN-EI-style Wau-Wau/Fuzz-Wah family. The archive should separate the Aria corporate brand from Aria Diamond-branded OEM examples where evidence supports a distinction.

### Shin-Ei / Companion / Honey
Current Shin-ei primary material states:
- Honey Co. FY-6 “Baby Crying” was released in 1967 and is described by Shin-ei as the first incarnation of the Super Fuzz.
- Shin-ei branded FY-6 followed in 1968.
- Shin-ei currently reproduces FY-6 and Companion FY-2 products.

The archive should preserve the chronology Honey → Shin-ei while separately modeling Companion FY-2 as its own family.

FY-2 candidate branded forms include Zenta, Jax, Tempo, Kimbara and Apollo. These should be promoted individually only after branding evidence is attached to the specific model record.

### Kurosawa “The Fuzz”
Effects Database documents a rare Japanese fuzz associated with Kurosawa Musical Instruments and a “Kurosawa / PRAT-7” board marking. It is described as a later Super-Fuzz-derived design with selectable tonal modes. Exact manufacturing source remains unclear. Archive status: rare model candidate, Japanese origin strong, OEM/builder uncertain.

### Maxon Fuzz Elements
Modern Maxon products are useful as a separate later-generation catalog branch and should not be confused with the historical Nisshin Onpa OEM history. Current Effects Database records include:
- FE10 Ether
- FA10 Air
- FF10 Fire
- FEA10 Earth
- FWA10 Water

These are modern Japanese products consciously referencing vintage fuzz/distortion families. They belong in the broader Maxon catalog but not in the historical 1960s OEM chronology.

## 2. Europe / Britain: obscure and OEM-heavy fuzz

### Italian Jen / Vox branch
High-priority connected records:
- Jen Fuzz
- Jen Tone Bender
- Vox V828 Tone Bender
- Vox V8281 Tone Bender
- Elka Fuzz
- Luxor Fuzz
- Nova Tone Bender
- Unicord Fuzz

Effects Database and Kit Rae's historical Big Muff research both identify Jen as a major Italian OEM manufacturer that produced effects for Vox, Dallas-Arbiter, Sound City, Elka and other brands. The Italian Vox Tone Bender branch should therefore be represented as an explicit manufacturing lineage rather than a pile of independent brands.

### Jen Big Muff-related branch
Kit Rae identifies the Jen Jumbo Fuzz, Sam Ash Jumbo Fuzz, Sam Ash Fuzzola II and Dallas-Arbiter Jumbo Fuzz as part of an early-1970s Italian Big Muff clone/OEM cluster associated with Jen Elettronica in Pescara.

This is a major bridge between British fuzz history and the later Italian Big Muff clone ecosystem. It merits its own future research file.

### Electronic Sounds / Gary Hurst / Italian production
Priority candidate records:
- Electronic Sounds Fuzz King
- Electronic Sounds UFO Fuzzman
- Electronic Sounds Sustain
- Electronic Sounds UFO Octa-Fuzz
- Electronic Sounds UFO Wah Fuzz Swell
- Electronic Sounds UFO Sustain
- Moreschi Octave Fuzz
- Moreschi Wah-Fuzz
- CBS-Arbiter Doubler
- CBS-Arbiter Sustain

Kit Rae's timeline describes Electronic Sounds Fuzz King, UFO Fuzzman and Electronic Sounds Sustain as Italian-made plastic-cased Big Muff/Supa Tonebender-related products from the mid-1970s and notes that the Electronic Sounds line was associated with Gary Hurst. Effects Database additionally groups the UFO Octa-Fuzz with Moreschi and CBS-Arbiter Doubler designs.

Moreschi is particularly useful for archive purposes because the company is primarily associated with accordions, yet it appears in a short-lived effects collaboration involving Gary Hurst. This is precisely the sort of non-obvious cross-industry manufacturing story The Dirt Archive should preserve.

Important editorial caution: some web descriptions are too confident about exact internal construction, transistor types, or one-to-one circuit identity. Public archive records should prefer externally useful identification distinctions and historical relationships while avoiding complete circuit documentation.

### Schaller / West German OEM branch
Priority candidates:
- Schaller Fuzz
- Höfner-branded Schaller-derived fuzz variants
- Kent-branded Schaller-derived fuzz variants
- Van Hall-branded Schaller-derived fuzz variants
- Blackfield-branded Schaller-derived fuzz variants

Effects Database describes Schaller fuzz production from the mid-1960s through the 1970s and identifies multiple OEM rebrands. Early germanium and later silicon examples appear in the historical record. The OEM network is more important than treating each retail badge as an unrelated pedal.

### Marshall / Sola Sound / Colorsound branch
Priority historical entries:
- Marshall Supa Fuzz
- JMI Supa Fuzz
- Sola Sound Tone Bender MkIII
- Colorsound Tone Bender Distortion
- RotoSound Fuzz Box MkII
- RotoSound Fuzz Box MkIII
- Park Fuzz Sound
- Carlsbro Fuzz
- CSL Super Fuzz
- Eurotec Black Box Fuzz Module
- Marque Super Distortion

Effects Database's Tone Bender relationship tree is a useful discovery map showing how the MkIII/Supa Fuzz family propagated across Sola Sound, Colorsound, Marshall, RotoSound, Park and other retail brands.

The archive should distinguish original period OEM/retail products from later boutique recreations that merely cite the lineage.

### RotoSound
A rare RotoSound Wow Fuzz was surfaced as a possible prototype, with the source itself using tentative language. Keep as a specimen candidate, not a verified production model.

RotoSound Fuzz Box Mk1.5 has a dedicated Effects Database entry documenting a period example attributed to Sola Sound manufacture. This is a strong candidate for the Sola Sound → RotoSound relational model.

### Selmer
Priority fuzz records:
- Selmer Buzz-Tone
- Selmer Fuzz-Wah

The Buzz-Tone is a late-1960s British fuzz product. Separate research into the Selmer Fuzz-Wah suggests version changes around the late 1960s / early 1970s. These should remain separate products and generations until the relationship is fully mapped.

### Gretsch
Priority records:
- Gretsch Controfuzz
- Gretsch Expandafuzz

These deserve individual model records. Effects Database shows the two as separate Gretsch fuzz products, with the Expandafuzz linked to later products that may be based on or influenced by its design.

## 3. American oddities and early plug-in fuzz

### Jordan / Sho-Sound Bosstone
The Jordan 1000 Boss Tone is a major archive target because it is not a conventional floor pedal. It is a guitar-mounted plug-in fuzz module.

Effects Database identifies multiple historical versions, including:
- Jordan Electronics, Alhambra, California
- Jordan Electronics, Pasadena, California
- Sho-Sound, Nashville, Tennessee

North Effects' historical account states that Jordan licensed production to Sho-Bud/Sho-Sound in Nashville and that multiple versions continued into the 1970s.

The archive should therefore create:
1. Jordan 1000 Boss Tone model
2. Jordan production generations
3. Sho-Sound / Sho-Bud licensed production relationship
4. Later pedal-format reinterpretations as separate modern descendants

### Walco
Walco Fuzz Tone Generator is a distinctive Japanese-made, belt-clip-style fuzz associated with late-1960s/1970s budget effects. It is a particularly useful reminder that “pedal” history includes small clip-on and instrument-mounted formats, not only floor boxes.

### Kay
Recent archival research in Tone Machines Blog materially changes the chronology of the Kay FUZ-1 Fuzz Tone. The blog reports a January 1974 Music Trades product announcement for the original three Kay “Sound Benders” series, including the orange FUZ-1, and argues against the longstanding late-1960s dating.

The same research says the Kay FUZ-1 was made in Japan, but its exact Japanese manufacturer remains unidentified. A Japanese-market Uecks Fuzz Tone is proposed as a possible clue because Uecks was associated with Ueki Gakki. Treat this entire builder relationship as unresolved.

This is a model example for the archive's future handling of corrected chronology: collector lore should be superseded when period documentary evidence is stronger.

### Gretsch / U.S. catalogs
Gretsch Controfuzz and Expandafuzz should be tracked as separate product records. Their relationship to later clones/derivatives can be stored without collapsing the original Gretsch models into the clone family.

## 4. Research rules reinforced by this pass

- Do not equate a retail badge with the manufacturer.
- Do not equate a shared circuit family with identical production history.
- Do not inherit dates from modern marketplace listings when period advertisements or catalogs contradict them.
- Treat “based on,” “same circuit,” “OEM,” and “clone” as different relationship types.
- Keep prototype and specimen reports separate from normal production models.
- Keep modern tributes and reissues separate from original historical products.
- Record component family changes only when they materially improve identification; omit full recipes/BOMs and reconstruction details.
- Preserve uncertainty explicitly, but avoid making evidence-state metadata dominate the eventual public-facing page.
